One recurring challenge in scientific research is that some of the most widely used statistical concepts are also among the most misunderstood.

As part of a series of workshops for PhD students and researchers at EMBL1, I developed training material to explain multiple testing, False Discovery Rate (FDR) control, and p-value miscalibration—concepts that researchers frequently encounter in practice but often find abstract, confusing, or intimidating.

Challenges

Modern biological experiments increasingly rely on high-throughput technologies that generate large datasets and create opportunities to test hundreds or thousands of hypotheses simultaneously. While this offers considerable scientific potential, it also introduces new statistical challenges that require additional caution during data analysis.

Multiple testing, False Discovery Rate (FDR) control, and p-value calibration are therefore central concepts in modern biological research, yet many researchers struggle to develop an intuitive understanding of what they mean in practice. Although encountered routinely, the reasoning behind them is often perceived as abstract, mathematically complex, or inaccessible.

The communication challenge was compounded by the highly heterogeneous statistical backgrounds of the workshop’s participants, ranging from researchers with minimal formal training in statistics to others with substantial quantitative experience.

Another challenge was therefore not simply to explain statistical procedures, but to help participants build intuition and confidence, understand what changes when moving from single to multiple hypothesis testing, and develop the reasoning needed to engage with these concepts independently after the session.

Approach

Rather than introducing these concepts through formulas and statistical procedures, I designed the workshop around visual reasoning and progressively reconstructed the intuition needed to understand multiple testing, False Discovery Rate control, and p-value calibration.

I used histograms as a consistent visual language throughout the presentation. While participants were generally familiar with these representations, they often lacked a clear understanding of how to interpret them statistically. Histograms therefore provided a natural starting point for introducing more sophisticated concepts without requiring entirely new visual tools.

Further design choices included:

  • structuring the explanation around the transition from single to multiple hypothesis testing, highlighting where intuitive reasoning begins to fail when the same reasoning is applied repeatedly across hundreds or thousands of tests;
  • introducing formulas without avoiding them, but only after participants have developed an intuitive understanding of the underlying concepts; and supporting them with colour coding, visual representations, and intuitive interpretations;
  • using visual reasoning to demonstrate how p-value calibration depends on underlying modelling assumptions, and how violations of these assumptions can fundamentally alter the interpretation of statistical results.

Strengths

  • Making abstract statistical concepts intuitive and non-intimidating;
  • Identifying and addressing common misconceptions in quantitative reasoning;
  • Using visual tools & reasoning (histograms, color, diagrams) to support conceptual understanding;
  • Communicating mathematical concepts without sacrificing rigour;
  • Designing structured learning experiences under time constraints (1h workshops);
  • Supporting audiences with highly heterogeneous quantitative backgrounds and levels of confidence in mathematics;
  • Connecting statistical theory to real-world data analysis practice.

Impact

By focusing on intuition before procedure, the workshop helped participants understand how multiple-testing corrections work, but also why they become necessary in the first place.

The goal of these workshops was not to turn experimental scientists into statisticians, but to provide them with the intuition and conceptual understanding needed to engage confidently with statistical analyses.

A limited understanding of statistical concepts can lead experimentalists either to mistrust data analysts or to blindly rely on them without fully understanding the reasoning behind the analysis. Neither situation is ideal.

By helping participants build intuition for commonly used statistical procedures, these workshops aimed to facilitate more informed discussions, more critical interpretation of results, and more effective collaboration between experimentalists and quantitative researchers.
