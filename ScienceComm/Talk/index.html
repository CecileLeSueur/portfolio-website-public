Beyond the Technical Contribution

After delivering an award winning 20-minute talk at ISMB 20231, I was selected to give a 10-minute presentation at ISMB 20242 introducing a methodological concept that combines a slight modification of GPMelt’s model [1] with a modified Thermal Proteome Profiling (TPP) experimental protocol to potentially double experimental throughput (see Section 7.4 of my PhD thesis [2]).

Beyond the technical contribution itself, the talk focused on the broader implications of reducing experimental costs and making advanced methodologies more accessible to a wider range of research groups. The communication challenge was to convey both the technical value and long-term potential of the idea to a diverse audience within only ten minutes.

Challenges

The talk targeted a diverse audience ranging from experimental biologists to computational researchers, most of whom were not specialists in Thermal Proteome Profiling.

The challenge was not only to explain the core idea clearly and accurately, but also to do so within a ten-minute conference format. This left very little time to introduce the scientific problem, explain the proposed solution, and persuade the audience that the idea mattered.

The communication challenge was further complicated by the structure of the message itself. The talk began with a highly specialised methodological problem before gradually revealing a broader question hidden beneath it: how reducing experimental costs could contribute to making advanced scientific methodologies more accessible. The key challenge was therefore to guide the audience from a niche technical concept to its wider implications without losing either scientific rigour or narrative momentum.

Approach

I structured the talk around a progressive reframing of the problem, moving from a specialised methodological challenge to a broader discussion about accessibility in scientific research.

Key design choices included:

  • introducing enough technical intuition to make the proposed solution credible without overwhelming a non-specialist audience;
  • prioritising the significance of the idea and what it could enable over a comprehensive methodological explanation;
  • using the methodological innovation as a tangible example of how statistical and experimental advances can reduce barriers to research;
  • designing a fast-paced narrative and slide structure that maintained audience engagement while delivering a complete and persuasive message within a ten-minute format.

Strengths

  • Communicating complex ideas under severe time constraints;
  • Maintaining audience engagement through narrative design and progressive revelation;
  • Identifying challenges faced by experimental researchers and addressing them through methodological innovation;
  • Framing technical innovations through broader scientific and societal implications;
  • Communicating the significance of ideas whose value lies primarily in what they could enable;
  • Designing messages that leave a lasting impression and encourage continued reflection beyond the presentation;
  • Aligning diverse scientific audiences around a common research bottleneck.

Impact

This talk was designed to communicate a methodological innovation just as much as to encourage the audience to think differently about the broader challenges it addressed. By connecting a specialised technical problem to questions of experimental cost and resource inequality, the presentation aimed to expand the conversation beyond methodology alone.

Ultimately, the objective was to show how seemingly small technical advances can have implications far beyond their immediate application, and to encourage researchers to consider how their own work might contribute to lowering barriers and broadening access to scientific discovery.

I came last year to ISMB and introduced my model, GPMelt. It's true that what I will present today might seem like a small incremental step of the modelling approach...

But thanks to a strong conceptual impact, I will show you that this small incremental step will take us to the moon! Let me first introduce you to the topic of Thermal stability and Thermal Proteome Profiling.

At low temperatures, proteins are folded, and as the temperature increases, bonds are disrupted and proteins start to denature and aggregate. We can follow this melting behaviour, which, according to the chemical denaturation theory, is expected to be sigmoidal.

An important fact about the thermal stability of proteins is that it’s not a fixed characteristic of a protein, but instead, is highly dependent on the protein’s surroundings and interactions. For example, upon interaction with small-molecule drugs, proteins can be stabilised or destabilised.

Based on this observation, the Cellular Thermal Shift assay has been developed to investigate drug targets and off-targets. Combining this assay with multiplexed quantitative Mass Spectrometry analysis...

...lead to the Temperature Range Thermal Proteome Profiling (TPP-TR) assay. Thanks to this assay, we have access - on a proteome-wide scale - to the melting behaviour at the tryptic peptide levels.

My PhD project was focused on developing a novel statistical framework for TPP-TR data analysis, which we called GPMelt.

To analyse TPP-TR data, we need to statistically assess the extent of the change in melting behaviour, and thus in thermal stability. GPMelt is not the first statistical framework for TPP, however, it presents some key new features which we will demonstrate in this talk.

The main idea is to combine Gaussian Process (GP) regression with multi-task learning to deduce the protein’s melting behaviour from the observed melting curves of the different replicates.

The reason why this feature is particularly important for TPP-TR data analysis is its capacity to provide robustness to outliers.

Indeed TPP-TR data inherently suffers from a low-signal to noise ratio, with replicates potentially tremendously varying.

Of course, a solution would be to obtain more replicates, but this would require increase the workload of our experimentalists...

Moreover, a main bottleneck is the high cost of TPP-TR experiments, mainly due to the number of samples to produce and the expensive TMT labelling and MS running time.

With this in mind, we started to wonder if we could use the improvement provided by the GPMelt analysis to help increase TPP-TR throughput.

Let’s consider a standard TPP-TR experiment, with two replicates and 8 measured temperatures.

Could we take advantage of our multi-task learning approach - which provides additional constraints and better shares replicates' information - for the replicates’ melting curves to be obtained even though we would measure only half the number of temperatures per replicate?

Well, yes and no. Especially, if we have half the number of observations, our protein’s fit (white curve) will show a much larger uncertainty (grey area around the curve). This is mainly because the replicates are measured in different MS runs and present some residual MS run batch effects that cannot be perfectly corrected by normalisation due to measurement errors specific to each MS run.

How can we solve this issue? We propose a minimalistic modification of GPMelt’s model. Currently, the observations are modelled by a function f, modelled as being drawn from a GP, and some iid normally distributed error.

We propose to explicitly model the offset “s”, modelling that observations across replicates are shifted differently (replicate 1 by s1 and replicate 2 by s2); but by the same amount within replicates (all observations of replicate 1 are shifted by s1).

To summarise, we propose to combine an updated GPMelt model...

...with a new protocol defined by redefined temperature and multiplexing.

All together is what we call “Offset-TPP”, a sample and cost-efficient TPP-TR assay and analysis.

Combining a minor modification of GPMelt model with an ingenious change of TPP’s protocol, allows to double throughput without increasing the cost, the workload or the input samples.

But in addition, Offset-TPP could finally enable analysis of precious samples, opening the way for new discoveries.

Now I would like to make you realise a benefit of this work on a broader scale.

Since it’s creation, TPP has been applied to the discovery of drugs targets and off-targets...

...and have been extended to a wide diversity of biological questions, leading to unprecedented discoveries regularly published in major research journals. However, TPP remains quite a small world, as it is restricted to scientists who can afford such expensive experiments.

By reducing by half the cost of TPP experiments, we make TPP discoveries affordable to more scientists, hence promoting more open and fairer Science.
